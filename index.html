01 — The Need

A domestic factory is no longer optional for Colombia.

Colombia's electrical grid is expanding faster than its capacity to equip it. Distribution and power transformers, prefabricated substations, switchgear and switching cells are largely imported, leaving projects exposed to long lead times, currency exposure and freight costs — while RETIE compliance (Resolution 40284 of 2026) raises the technical bar for every installation. Local manufacturing turns those weaknesses into advantages.

⚡

Grid & renewable expansion

Solar, wind and storage projects across La Guajira, Magdalena and the Coffee Axis all require medium-voltage transformation that today arrives on ships and customs queues.

🏭

Import dependence

Transformers and distribution equipment are among the electrical items Colombia imports most. Every unit shipped in is a unit that could be built here — with local labour and local content.

📐

RETIE as a market barrier

Resolution 40284/2026 tightens verification, testing and documentation requirements. A local factory shortens the certification loop and guarantees traceable compliance.

🏗️

Industry & mining demand

Mining, cement, beverage and food processors in Antioquia and Bogotá need fast replacement cycles — impossible to serve reliably from a 30-day ocean lead time.

🚚

Lead time as competitive edge

A plant in Colombia converts a 60–90 day import cycle into a 2–4 week local delivery, with after-sales service and spare parts in the same country.

🌎

A hub for the region

From Colombia, Taili gains duty-advantaged access to Andean and Latin American markets, operated by a team that already trades across the region.

05 — Market Study

Where to build: the case for Medellín and Colombia.

Two questions define the factory location decision: where inside Colombia maximises domestic reach and operational advantage, and whether Colombia itself is the right platform to serve the broader Latin American market. Both answers point in the same direction.

5.1

Inside Colombia

Medellín is the optimal location for a transformer factory.

🏭

Consolidated electrical cluster

Medellín hosts more than 900 companies in the electrical value chain, grouped under the Clúster de Energía Sostenible — the first energy cluster in Colombia, founded in 2006 by the Cámara de Comercio de Medellín. It generates over 30,000 direct jobs and 10,000 indirect in the sector.

🎓

Deep engineering talent pool

Universidad Nacional (Medellín campus), UPB, EAFIT and ITM produce hundreds of electrical and mechanical engineers per year. Key institutions such as EPM, ISA, ISAGEN and CIDET are headquartered here, creating a dense ecosystem of technical professionals.

⚡

Industrial energy infrastructure

EPM (Empresas Públicas de Medellín) supplies reliable, competitively priced electricity — critical for energy-intensive manufacturing. Antioquia generates over 30% of Colombia's hydroelectric output, giving local industry a structural cost advantage over other regions.

🛣️

Central logistics position

Medellín sits at the geographic heart of Colombia's road network. The 4G highway programme connects it to Bogotá (8 h), Cali (7 h), the Caribbean ports of Barranquilla and Cartagena, and the Pacific port of Buenaventura — covering all major domestic markets within one day by road.

✈️

Air cargo gateway

José María Córdova International Airport (Rionegro) is Colombia's second-largest cargo airport and handles regular freight connections to Miami, Bogotá and Panama — enabling fast dispatch of urgent equipment across the country and regionally.

🏗️

Industrial land & free zones

Antioquia has active free zones (Zona Franca Rionegro, ZF Urabá) offering a reduced corporate tax rate of 20% vs. the general 35%, plus simplified customs for imported components — a direct benefit for a factory that imports steel, core laminations and insulating materials from China.

🏙️

Proximity to the main demand centres

Medellín and the metropolitan area of Valle de Aburrá concentrate the heaviest concentration of industry in Colombia: mining (Bajo Cauca, Nordeste), cement, beverages, textiles and a fast-growing real estate market — all intensive consumers of distribution and power transformers.

🌿

RETIE compliance & local certification

CIDET (Centro de Investigación y Desarrollo Tecnológico del Sector Eléctrico), the official laboratory for RETIE electrical certification in Colombia, is based in Medellín. Having the certifying body minutes from the factory compresses the approval cycle from months to weeks.

5.2

Regional Platform

Colombia is the natural hub to serve South America, Central America and Mexico.

Once the Medellín factory is operational, the same infrastructure, workforce and certification base serve an export market of over 500 million people across 20+ countries — with Colombia holding binding trade agreements with virtually all of them.

📜

Trade agreements with the whole region

Colombia holds active free-trade agreements with Mexico (TLC-G3, in force since 1995), the Northern Triangle of Central America — Guatemala, El Salvador and Honduras (in force 2009–2010) — and the Andean Community (CAN: Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia). Exports to these markets face zero or near-zero tariffs on manufactured electrical goods.

🌎

Geographic centre of the hemisphere

Colombia is the only country in South America with coastlines on both the Pacific and the Atlantic (Caribbean). Medellín is within 3 hours by air from Miami, Mexico City, Lima, Quito, Caracas and Panama City — making it the most connected manufacturing location in the region.

📈

South America power transformer market

The South American power transformer market is projected to grow from USD 1.16 billion in 2025 to USD 1.53 billion by 2030 at a CAGR of 5.6% (MarketsandMarkets, 2025), driven by infrastructure investment and energy modernisation across Brazil, Colombia, Chile, Peru and Argentina.

🇲🇽

Mexico: a major market with a direct TLC

Mexico is the second-largest economy in Latin America and one of the fastest-growing markets for electrical infrastructure. The TLC-G3 with Colombia eliminates tariffs on industrial goods, and Colombia's Spanish-speaking technical teams can operate seamlessly in the Mexican market without cultural or language barriers.

🔌

Central America: infrastructure gap = opportunity

Guatemala, Costa Rica, Panama, El Salvador and Honduras are in the middle of massive electrification and grid-expansion programmes. Local manufacturing capacity is almost zero; transformers today arrive from the USA or Europe at a premium. A Colombian factory can deliver in 10–15 days by sea or road through Panama.

☀️

Renewable energy wave across the continent

The energy transition is driving transformer demand across the region: solar parks in Chile and Peru, wind farms in Mexico's Oaxaca corridor, mini-hydro in Ecuador and Bolivia, and large-scale solar in Colombia's Guajira — all require medium-voltage transformation equipment that today is largely imported from Asia or Europe.

🚢

Dual-ocean export logistics

From Medellín, freight moves to Buenaventura (Pacific, 8 h by road) for shipments to Chile, Peru, Ecuador and Mexico's Pacific coast — and to Cartagena or Barranquilla (Caribbean, 10 h) for Central America, the Caribbean and the US Gulf Coast. No other South American manufacturing city has this dual-ocean access.

🏆

Colombia already exports transformers

Colombia has a proven track record: Colombian-made transformers (Siemens Energy Medellín plant) were exported in 2025 in what was described as the largest single transformer export from Colombia — shipped to the United States from the port of Cartagena. The export infrastructure and know-how already exist.
